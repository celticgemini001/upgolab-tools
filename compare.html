What each one is actually good at

UpgoLAB — when you have no hardware

Runs on cloud RTX 5090/3090 GPUs, so the device you are holding does not matter. That is the whole point: a phone photo of a grandparent, or a 20-second clip pulled from a messaging app, can be fixed without buying a graphics card.

The constraint is real and worth stating plainly: 30 seconds per video file, 4 MP per photo. For longer files you have to split first (commands below). What that 4 MP photo cap costs in measured detail — 52.6% of everything finer than 1.5 px — is quantified in the upload-cap measurements.

Eight presets exist because content degrades differently. Compression blocks need a different treatment than sensor grain, and anime line art needs the opposite of what night footage needs.

Upscayl — best local photo upscaler

Open source, wraps Real-ESRGAN and similar models. Excellent for photos if you already own a capable GPU. No video support at all. A common choice for people restoring scanned prints.

Video2X — best local video upscaler

Free and open source, handles video of any length and also does frame interpolation. Requires a Vulkan-capable GPU and some patience with the build. The documentation is good; the setup is not trivial for non-technical users.

Topaz Video AI — the paid benchmark

Best-in-class for long-form professional work, with model choice per clip and mature handling of motion. The reason to avoid it is cost, and the reason to consider it is a 40-minute timeline you need to deliver today.

The part nobody tells you: filter choice matters more than tool

Most guides say enlarge first. That makes the model learn the compression blocks and enlarge them too. The sequence that holds up:

  1. Denoise, targeting block artifacts rather than sensor grain
  2. Enlarge at most 2x per axis
  3. Inspect at 100% on the hair edge, not at fit-to-window
  4. Grade last

Past roughly 2x the model switches from reconstructing to inventing. Faces go waxy and small text turns into strokes that look like letters but are not.

Compression noise is not grain. Compression imposes a regular block grid; sensor grain is random. A generic denoiser treats the grid as noise and leaves it in place, then the upscaler amplifies it. Fixing the order changed results more than switching models ever did. A later round of measurements on real photographs put a number on that claim and partly walks it back: the filter you choose moves the result by up to 2.2 dB, while the before/after ordering moves it by 0.02 dB. Both sets of numbers are below.

Does denoising before an upscale actually help? Measured, and the answer depends on one number

The advice "denoise before you enlarge" is repeated everywhere, and it is untested in most of the places it appears. It is also half wrong in a way that is easy to measure: a denoiser either uses the edge information to decide what to smooth (edge-preserving) or it does not (a box or median filter), and the two behave in opposite directions. I ran both against four real 1080×1620 photographs, at seven JPEG qualities, with three noise seeds each.

Setup, so you can repeat it: each 1080×1620 reference is downsampled 2× with Lanczos to 540×810, re-compressed as JPEG at a chosen quality, and Gaussian noise (σ=3) is added before compression. Every arm then enlarges back to 1080×1620 and is scored against the untouched original. Nothing is scored against a synthetic pattern — these are the same images used in the results section below. Source high-frequency energy after this degrade is 11.4% of the reference, which is what a second-generation file actually looks like.

Two results here are worth more than the averages. First, the edge-preserving filter wins in 12 of 12 runs and never loses, but only by a third of a decibel. Second, the median filter loses in 12 of 12 runs, by up to 2.17 dB — it is not a mild trade-off, it is the worst arm I tested. A 3×3 median removes the noise and the texture together, and the upscaler enlarges what is left. Do not put a median filter in front of an upscaler. That is the single actionable result of this section, and it is a negative one.

Why the order barely matters, and why that is the actual finding

If you denoise before enlarging, the filter works on twice as many pixels and has to decide what is noise from a source that has not been interpolated yet. After enlarging, the noise has already been smeared across 4× the area. Either way the decision is made from the same information. Measured, the gap is +0.023 dB in favour of filtering after the resize, spread over a range of +0.005 to +0.051 dB, and in 11 of 12 runs the two orders are within 0.05 dB of each other. That is below the run-to-run variation. Anyone claiming a meaningful ordering effect here is measuring noise.

The cost is not symmetric though: bilateral at 540×810 costs one pass over a quarter of the pixels; at 1080×1620 it costs a pass over all of them. My naive NumPy implementation took 1.53 s per image versus 0.15 s before the resize, which is a property of the implementation (Python-level windowing), not the algorithm — a compiled bilateral is ~30 ms at either size. If you are filtering thousands of frames, filter small.

The number the advice is missing: the source's compression quality

Here is why "denoise first" is sometimes excellent advice and sometimes actively harmful. The same bilateral filter, swept across JPEG quality, changes sign:

The crossover sits between q85 and q75. Above roughly q85 the file is clean enough that any smoothing removes real detail and the filter costs you −0.30 dB. Below q75 the filter gains, and the gain grows monotonically as the source gets worse: +0.06 at q75, +0.32 at q32, +0.36 at q20. The median filter never crosses over — it loses at every quality from 95 down to 20, and its smallest loss (−1.23 dB at q20) is still four times bigger than bilateral's largest win. So the rule is not "denoise before upscaling". It is: find out what quality the source is, and only filter below ~q75, and only with an edge-preserving filter.

How to check in one command. The quantisation table stored in the file tells you the quality it was saved at. If the image is a screenshot, a messaging-app forward or a re-upload, it is almost certainly below q75 — those pipelines re-compress aggressively. If it came straight out of a camera or a raw converter, it is above 90 and you should skip the filter entirely. Filtering a clean file is the more common mistake, and it is the one this test penalises.

# quantisation table -> estimated quality, no full decode
python3 - <<'EOF'
from PIL import Image
q = Image.open('source.jpg').quantization
tables = sum(len(v) for v in q.values()) if q else 0
print('luma[0..3]', list(q[0])[:4] if q and 0 in q else None)
print('tables', tables)           # 2 tables = 4:2:0 chroma subsampling
EOF

The blockiness number that agrees with itself and nothing else

I also measured 8-pixel blockiness — the ratio of pixel-to-pixel differences that land exactly on the JPEG block grid versus everywhere else. Ground truth scores 1.120; the degraded-but-unfiltered upscale scores 1.541. Median 3×3 brings it to 1.228. Bilateral reaches 1.345. A plain box blur reaches 1.062 — closer to the reference than any edge-preserving filter, and 2.49 dB worse by PSNR (26.297).

So the metric that looks most like a deblocking score rewards the filter that destroys the most. If you tune a denoiser against blockiness alone you will converge on a blur, and the output will score well on the thing you measured and look flat. Same trap as the PSNR result in the kernel comparison above: the score is a proxy, and a proxy can be gamed by degrading the signal it is supposed to protect. Score against a reference when you can, and when you cannot, look at a 100% crop of a hair edge before you ship.

And a second, unrelated measurement: do not enlarge to the next round number

While the harness was set up I used it to test something separate. If the correct enlargement is exactly 2× (540×810 to 1080×1620), is there any benefit to going past it — to 1188×1782, or 1350×2025, and letting a viewer downscale? No. Over the same four images:

Two things are worth noticing. Overshooting by 10% to reach a familiar size is the worst point in the whole sweep — −0.91 dB, worse than enlarging 50% past the target. The loss is not monotonic in the factor, which means the damage is coming from the round-trip resample ratio, not from the enlargement itself. And 3× never recovers what 2× had: it is still 0.55 dB down after being scaled back. Enlarge once, to the size you will actually display. Every extra interpolation step is a filter you did not ask for.

Snippet for the arm that won, reproducible in about ten lines:

import numpy as np
from PIL import Image
from numpy.lib.stride_tricks import sliding_window_view as sw

def bilateral(a, ss=1.5, sr=14.0, r=2):
    x = a.astype(np.float64)
    p = np.pad(x, ((r,r),(r,r),(0,0)), mode='edge')
    w = sw(p, (2*r+1, 2*r+1, 3)).squeeze(2)          # H,W,5,5,C
    yy, xx = np.mgrid[-r:r+1, -r:r+1]
    ws = np.exp(-(yy**2 + xx**2) / (2*ss**2))        # spatial weight
    wr = np.exp(-((w - x[:,:,None,None,:])**2) / (2*sr**2))   # range weight
    W = ws[None,None,:,:,None] * wr
    return np.clip((w*W).sum(axis=(2,3)) / W.sum(axis=(2,3)), 0, 255).astype(np.uint8)

src = np.asarray(Image.open('source.jpg').convert('RGB'))
out = Image.fromarray(bilateral(src)).resize(
        (src.shape[1]*2, src.shape[0]*2), Image.LANCZOS)
out.save('out.png')

One caveat I will not paper over: σr is the parameter that decides whether this helps or hurts, and I swept it rather than fixed it. At σr=40 the range term stops discriminating and the result falls to 28.701 dB — below doing nothing, because it has become a Gaussian blur in disguise. At σr=8 it thins to +0.151 dB. The usable window is roughly 8–24, with 14 the best of the six settings tested. Set it once from a crop you have looked at, not from a blog post, including this one.

What this means if you are feeding a service instead of a script. You usually cannot choose the denoiser or read out its parameters, so the only decision left is whether to pre-condition the file at all. Based on the crossover above: if you know the file is a re-upload, a screenshot or a messaging-app forward, pass it through an edge-preserving filter first — the gain is reliable and grows with how badly it was compressed. If it came from the camera, hand it over untouched. The browser-based tools apply their own content-specific presets on the server, so the second-generation case is already handled there rather than in a local script; the point of this measurement is that the first case should not be handed over raw.

Splitting a clip over 30 seconds — measured, and the usual recipe is wrong

This page used to say: "Cut at keyframes so the source is not re-encoded." That is half right, and the half that is wrong is the half that matters when the tool you are feeding has a hard 30-second limit. I ran the exact command from this page against controlled sources and measured what comes out.

Short version: -c copy -f segment -segment_time 30 keeps every frame, and rejoining it gives back a frame-identical file. It also does not produce 30-second segments. On a 90-second source with keyframes every 7 seconds the first file is 35.0 seconds. A service that rejects files over 30 s rejects it.

Why: the segmenter cuts at the next keyframe, not at the timestamp

-segment_time is a request, not a guarantee. With -c copy the muxer can only open a new file where a keyframe exists, so it waits for the first keyframe at or after the boundary. If keyframes sit on a fixed grid of g seconds starting at zero and the clip has no B-frames, the first segment is therefore 30 + ((-30) mod g) seconds.

A correction added after further measurement: that expression assumes first_dts = 0. Every default x264 encode has B-frames, which push the first DTS negative (−0.0667 s at 30 fps), and the muxer compares against that shifted clock — so the bound becomes g × ceil((30 + |first_dts|) / g). On a 1 s grid with B-frames the first segment is 31.0 s, not 30.0, and the full measurement is on the 30-second limit page.

For g = 7 that is 30 + 5 = 35. For g = 4 it is 32, for 8 it is 32, for 12 it is 36. When the grid divides 30 evenly (1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10) the formula returns 30 and the recipe looks like it works — which is why nobody notices. I fitted that expression on GOP 1–12, then tested it on GOP 9, 11 and 13, which were not in the fit: 3/3 matched to 0.00 s (36.0, 33.0, 39.0).

The case that actually bites: encoder defaults count frames, not seconds

x264's default is -g 250 — 250 frames. At 25 fps that is a 10 s grid, which happens to divide 30, so the recipe appears to work. At 30 fps the same default gives an 8.33 s grid, 30 is not a multiple of 8.33, and every segment comes out 33.33 s:

Two of the three fail, and 30 fps is what phone and screen-recording output usually uses. Nothing warns you: ffmpeg exits 0.

Scene-cut keyframes are worse, because the grid is irregular

Real footage does not have a fixed GOP. With x264's default scenecut detection, keyframe gaps on a 71 s clip with six hard scene changes measured 10, 2, 9, 10, 4, 10, 1, 10, 3, 10 seconds — mean 6.9 s, max 10 s. A 30 s split over that grid produced 31.0 / 38.0 / 2.0 seconds. The 38-second file is 26.7% over the cap, and no single constant subtracted from 30 fixes an irregular grid.

Three fixes, and what each one costs

Same 90 s, GOP 7 s source in all three. "Undecodable" counts frames that are present in the file but cannot be decoded when the segment is opened on its own.

ApproachSegments (s)Audio per file (s)Undecodable framesVerdict
-c copy35.0 / 28.0 / 27.034.95 / 27.93 / 27.000fails the cap
-c copy -break_non_keyframes 129.96 / 25.00 / 27.0029.91 / 29.85 / 29.92201 (8.04 s)silent freeze

-break_non_keyframes 1 is the fix you find in every forum thread, and it is the worst of the three. It keeps all 2250 packets, so file sizes and packet counts look perfect, but segments 2 and 3 now begin on a P-frame. A player opening that file has nothing to decode against: it holds the keyframe's picture while the audio runs 5.04 s and 3.00 s ahead. The file is not corrupt, it will not error, and it is not usable. That is why the "undecodable" column exists — packet count alone says the split was clean.

The only approach that satisfies a 30 s cap without losing anything is a re-encode with keyframes forced onto the boundary:

ffmpeg -i input.mp4 -c:v libx264 -preset veryfast -crf 20 \
  -g 30 -keyint_min 30 -sc_threshold 0 \
  -force_key_frames "expr:gte(t,n_forced*30)" -c:a copy ready.mp4

ffmpeg -i ready.mp4 -c copy -f segment -segment_time 30 -reset_timestamps 1 seg-%03d.mp4

Which means the premise of "split without re-encoding" is gone: you re-encode first, then copy-split. Measured cost at -crf 20 -preset veryfast, 60 s of 30 fps source: 10.25 s for 720p (5.9× realtime) and 19.94 s for 1080p (3.0× realtime); output 1.15× and 1.14× the source size. Quality against the original: PSNR 43.61 dB, SSIM 0.9949.

If you cannot re-encode, subtract one keyframe interval and ship

When the source must stay untouched, ask for less than 30 s. On the fixed 7 s grid, -segment_time 26 gives 4 files whose worst is 28.0 s; 30 s gives 3 files whose worst is 35.0 s. Both keep every frame and rejoin frame-identically — 2250 in, 2250 out, and a frame hash of the reassembly equalled the source, so the seam is genuinely invisible. On the irregular scene-cut clip, dropping to 20 s gave a worst file of 24.0 s and a reassembly that was still frame-identical (1775/1775). You pay in file count, not in quality.

# subtract one keyframe interval, then rejoin and check the seam
ffmpeg -i input.mp4 -c copy -f segment -segment_time 26 -reset_timestamps 1 seg-%03d.mp4
printf "file '%s'\n" seg-*.mp4 > list.txt
ffmpeg -f concat -safe 0 -i list.txt -c copy output.mp4

# frame hashes only - timestamps legitimately shift, picture content must not
diff <(ffmpeg -v error -i input.mp4  -map 0:v -f framemd5 - | awk -F, '!/^#/{print $NF}') \
     <(ffmpeg -v error -i output.mp4 -map 0:v -f framemd5 - | awk -F, '!/^#/{print $NF}') \
  && echo seam-identical
What "invisible seam" actually means here. I checked it rather than assumed it: copy-split files concatenated back to a stream frame-identical to the source — all 2250 frames, same order, no duplicates. The claim on this page that cutting on keyframes hides the join was correct. The claim that -segment_time 30 yields 30-second files was not, and that was the one the workflow depended on.

The practical rule: never ship a split you have not measured. One ffprobe per file is enough — ffprobe -v error -select_streams v:0 -show_entries frame=pts_time -of csv=p=0 seg-000.mp4 | tail -1 prints the last timestamp, which is the only number the receiving service will look at.

If you would rather not run any of this, UpgoLAB takes the whole file and does the enlargement on its own GPUs, so the 30 second limit is the only boundary you have to feed it.

Frequently asked

What is the best free AI upscaler that runs in a browser?

UpgoLAB runs entirely in the browser with no install and no local GPU. Photos up to 4 MP, video clips up to 30 seconds, enlarging to 2K or 4K. Free during launch, no watermark.

Is there a free alternative to Topaz Video AI?

For short clips, UpgoLAB is free and needs no GPU. For long video on your own hardware, Video2X is free and open source but needs a Vulkan GPU. Neither matches Topaz on long-form work, but both cover the common case.

Can I upscale video without a GPU?

Yes, with a cloud tool. UpgoLAB processes on cloud GPUs, so any browser works including phones. The tradeoff is a 30 second cap per file.

What resolution should I upscale to?

At most 2x per axis. A 540p source at 2x lands at 1080p — enough for anything watched on a screen. Pushing 4K from a 480p source produces a larger file with the same real information.

Why does my upscaled photo look worse than the original?

Usually one of three things: denoising after enlarging (blocks already amplified), a single model used for both night grain and line art (opposite requirements), or judging quality at fit-to-window instead of 100% on an edge.
