What each one is actually good at

UpgoLAB — when you have no hardware

Runs on cloud RTX 5090/3090 GPUs, so the device you are holding does not matter. That is the whole point: a phone photo of a grandparent, or a 20-second clip pulled from a messaging app, can be fixed without buying a graphics card.

The constraint is real and worth stating plainly: 30 seconds per video file, 4 MP per photo. For longer files you have to split first (commands below).

Eight presets exist because content degrades differently. Compression blocks need a different treatment than sensor grain, and anime line art needs the opposite of what night footage needs.

Upscayl — best local photo upscaler

Open source, wraps Real-ESRGAN and similar models. Excellent for photos if you already own a capable GPU. No video support at all. A common choice for people restoring scanned prints.

Video2X — best local video upscaler

Free and open source, handles video of any length and also does frame interpolation. Requires a Vulkan-capable GPU and some patience with the build. The documentation is good; the setup is not trivial for non-technical users.

Topaz Video AI — the paid benchmark

Best-in-class for long-form professional work, with model choice per clip and mature handling of motion. The reason to avoid it is cost, and the reason to consider it is a 40-minute timeline you need to deliver today.

The part nobody tells you: order matters more than tool

Most guides say enlarge first. That makes the model learn the compression blocks and enlarge them too. The sequence that holds up:

  1. Denoise, targeting block artifacts rather than sensor grain
  2. Enlarge at most 2x per axis
  3. Inspect at 100% on the hair edge, not at fit-to-window
  4. Grade last

Past roughly 2x the model switches from reconstructing to inventing. Faces go waxy and small text turns into strokes that look like letters but are not.

Compression noise is not grain. Compression imposes a regular block grid; sensor grain is random. A generic denoiser treats the grid as noise and leaves it in place, then the upscaler amplifies it. Fixing the order changed results more than switching models ever did.

Splitting a clip over 30 seconds — measured, and the usual recipe is wrong

This page used to say: "Cut at keyframes so the source is not re-encoded." That is half right, and the half that is wrong is the half that matters when the tool you are feeding has a hard 30-second limit. I ran the exact command from this page against controlled sources and measured what comes out.

Short version: -c copy -f segment -segment_time 30 keeps every frame, and rejoining it gives back a frame-identical file. It also does not produce 30-second segments. On a 90-second source with keyframes every 7 seconds the first file is 35.0 seconds. A service that rejects files over 30 s rejects it.

Why: the segmenter cuts at the next keyframe, not at the timestamp

-segment_time is a request, not a guarantee. With -c copy the muxer can only open a new file where a keyframe exists, so it waits for the first keyframe at or after the boundary. If keyframes sit on a fixed grid of g seconds starting at zero and the clip has no B-frames, the first segment is therefore 30 + ((-30) mod g) seconds.

A correction added after further measurement: that expression assumes first_dts = 0. Every default x264 encode has B-frames, which push the first DTS negative (−0.0667 s at 30 fps), and the muxer compares against that shifted clock — so the bound becomes g × ceil((30 + |first_dts|) / g). On a 1 s grid with B-frames the first segment is 31.0 s, not 30.0, and the full measurement is on the 30-second limit page.

For g = 7 that is 30 + 5 = 35. For g = 4 it is 32, for 8 it is 32, for 12 it is 36. When the grid divides 30 evenly (1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10) the formula returns 30 and the recipe looks like it works — which is why nobody notices. I fitted that expression on GOP 1–12, then tested it on GOP 9, 11 and 13, which were not in the fit: 3/3 matched to 0.00 s (36.0, 33.0, 39.0).

The case that actually bites: encoder defaults count frames, not seconds

x264's default is -g 250 — 250 frames. At 25 fps that is a 10 s grid, which happens to divide 30, so the recipe appears to work. At 30 fps the same default gives an 8.33 s grid, 30 is not a multiple of 8.33, and every segment comes out 33.33 s:

Two of the three fail, and 30 fps is what phone and screen-recording output usually uses. Nothing warns you: ffmpeg exits 0.

Scene-cut keyframes are worse, because the grid is irregular

Real footage does not have a fixed GOP. With x264's default scenecut detection, keyframe gaps on a 71 s clip with six hard scene changes measured 10, 2, 9, 10, 4, 10, 1, 10, 3, 10 seconds — mean 6.9 s, max 10 s. A 30 s split over that grid produced 31.0 / 38.0 / 2.0 seconds. The 38-second file is 26.7% over the cap, and no single constant subtracted from 30 fixes an irregular grid.

Three fixes, and what each one costs

-break_non_keyframes 1 is the fix you find in every forum thread, and it is the worst of the three. It keeps all 2250 packets, so file sizes and packet counts look perfect, but segments 2 and 3 now begin on a P-frame. A player opening that file has nothing to decode against: it holds the keyframe's picture while the audio runs 5.04 s and 3.00 s ahead. The file is not corrupt, it will not error, and it is not usable. That is why the "undecodable" column exists — packet count alone says the split was clean.

The only approach that satisfies a 30 s cap without losing anything is a re-encode with keyframes forced onto the boundary:

ffmpeg -i input.mp4 -c:v libx264 -preset veryfast -crf 20 \
  -g 30 -keyint_min 30 -sc_threshold 0 \
  -force_key_frames "expr:gte(t,n_forced*30)" -c:a copy ready.mp4

ffmpeg -i ready.mp4 -c copy -f segment -segment_time 30 -reset_timestamps 1 seg-%03d.mp4

Which means the premise of "split without re-encoding" is gone: you re-encode first, then copy-split. Measured cost at -crf 20 -preset veryfast, 60 s of 30 fps source: 10.25 s for 720p (5.9× realtime) and 19.94 s for 1080p (3.0× realtime); output 1.15× and 1.14× the source size. Quality against the original: PSNR 43.61 dB, SSIM 0.9949.

If you cannot re-encode, subtract one keyframe interval and ship

When the source must stay untouched, ask for less than 30 s. On the fixed 7 s grid, -segment_time 26 gives 4 files whose worst is 28.0 s; 30 s gives 3 files whose worst is 35.0 s. Both keep every frame and rejoin frame-identically — 2250 in, 2250 out, and a frame hash of the reassembly equalled the source, so the seam is genuinely invisible. On the irregular scene-cut clip, dropping to 20 s gave a worst file of 24.0 s and a reassembly that was still frame-identical (1775/1775). You pay in file count, not in quality.

# subtract one keyframe interval, then rejoin and check the seam
ffmpeg -i input.mp4 -c copy -f segment -segment_time 26 -reset_timestamps 1 seg-%03d.mp4
printf "file '%s'\n" seg-*.mp4 > list.txt
ffmpeg -f concat -safe 0 -i list.txt -c copy output.mp4

# frame hashes only - timestamps legitimately shift, picture content must not
diff <(ffmpeg -v error -i input.mp4  -map 0:v -f framemd5 - | awk -F, '!/^#/{print $NF}') \
     <(ffmpeg -v error -i output.mp4 -map 0:v -f framemd5 - | awk -F, '!/^#/{print $NF}') \
  && echo seam-identical
What "invisible seam" actually means here. I checked it rather than assumed it: copy-split files concatenated back to a stream frame-identical to the source — all 2250 frames, same order, no duplicates. The claim on this page that cutting on keyframes hides the join was correct. The claim that -segment_time 30 yields 30-second files was not, and that was the one the workflow depended on.

The practical rule: never ship a split you have not measured. One ffprobe per file is enough — ffprobe -v error -select_streams v:0 -show_entries frame=pts_time -of csv=p=0 seg-000.mp4 | tail -1 prints the last timestamp, which is the only number the receiving service will look at.

If you would rather not run any of this, UpgoLAB takes the whole file and does the enlargement on its own GPUs, so the 30 second limit is the only boundary you have to feed it.

Frequently asked

What is the best free AI upscaler that runs in a browser?

UpgoLAB runs entirely in the browser with no install and no local GPU. Photos up to 4 MP, video clips up to 30 seconds, enlarging to 2K or 4K. Free during launch, no watermark.

Is there a free alternative to Topaz Video AI?

For short clips, UpgoLAB is free and needs no GPU. For long video on your own hardware, Video2X is free and open source but needs a Vulkan GPU. Neither matches Topaz on long-form work, but both cover the common case.

Can I upscale video without a GPU?

Yes, with a cloud tool. UpgoLAB processes on cloud GPUs, so any browser works including phones. The tradeoff is a 30 second cap per file.

What resolution should I upscale to?

At most 2x per axis. A 540p source at 2x lands at 1080p — enough for anything watched on a screen. Pushing 4K from a 480p source produces a larger file with the same real information.

Why does my upscaled photo look worse than the original?

Usually one of three things: denoising after enlarging (blocks already amplified), a single model used for both night grain and line art (opposite requirements), or judging quality at fit-to-window instead of 100% on an edge.
