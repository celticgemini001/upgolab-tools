The measurement that organises everything else

Interpolation methods ranked by PSNR are ranked almost exactly backwards by detail retention. On a 4× upscale of a Siemens-star test pattern:

MethodPSNRHF detail retained
nearest24.60 dB25.4%
bilinear25.68 dB3.9%
bicubic26.27 dB2.1%
lanczos26.47 dB1.3%

At 2× instead of 4×, Lanczos retains 21.5% instead of 1.3%. Halving the scale factor gains roughly 16× more surviving detail — far more than any choice of kernel. That single fact reframes most upscaling decisions.

Which knob actually decides whether detail survives?

The nine guides above each change one thing at a time. That is fine for explaining a mechanism and useless for ranking causes, because the knobs interact. So the next test ran a full factorial: 3 scale factors × 3 kernels × 4 content classes = 36 cells, on 1024×1024 sources. Each source was downscaled with a box filter (no ringing introduced before the test), then upscaled back and scored for high-frequency retention against its own ground truth.

The point of a factorial is not the 36 numbers. It is that you can attribute the variance between them to a cause. Taking the main-effect sum of squares on log retention:

Source of variationLevelsShare of variance
Scale factor (2× / 3× / 4×)370.1%
Content class (text, line art, portrait, product edge)417.3%
Kernel (bilinear / bicubic / lanczos)30.1%

Choosing the kernel — the decision every upscaling UI puts first, in a dropdown — accounts for one part in a thousand of the variation in surviving detail. The scale factor accounts for seven hundred times more. Content class sits in between, which is why presets named after content ("photo", "anime", "text") beat presets named after filters.

Mean high-frequency retention by content class and factor:

Content class2×3×4×
Text / UI37.43%22.60%7.47%
Line art30.01%16.27%8.37%
Portrait-like36.81%24.82%21.27%
Product edge28.00%14.14%7.13%
All cells33.06%19.46%11.06%

The kernel ranking flips, which is why it cannot be trusted

If kernel mattered, the best kernel would be the best kernel. Across the twelve cells at each factor, averaged:

FactorbilinearbicubiclanczosWinner
2×28.02%35.98%35.19%bicubic
3×20.43%19.55%18.38%bilinear
4×11.92%10.68%10.57%bilinear

Bicubic wins at 2× by 2.4 points and loses at 4× by 1.3. The ordering is not a property of the kernel; it is a property of the kernel at that factor on that content. Every "which interpolation is best" comparison that fixes one factor is reporting an interaction term as if it were a main effect.

The interaction is visible directly as the spread between kernels inside a single cell. At 2× the spread reaches 10.27 points on text; at 4× the same content has only 2.37 points of kernel spread. The higher the factor, the less the filter choice can matter, because there is less information left to preserve differently.

Two results that contradict the usual advice

RMSE does not track detail retention. Correlating log retention against RMSE across all 36 cells gives r = −0.30. A weaker relationship than that is hard to produce from numbers measuring the same images. At 4× on line art, lanczos has the lowest RMSE (66.22) and the highest retention (8.66%); at 4× on product edges, bilinear has both the highest RMSE (6.65) and the highest retention (9.19%). If you rank methods by error you will pick differently depending on the content, which means the metric is not measuring what the eye uses.

Step size beats every other choice, and the curve is not linear. Going 3× → 4× costs a mean of 8.4 points of retention (19.46% → 11.06%); going 2× → 3× costs 13.6 points. The first doubling is the expensive one. Practical reading: if a target size is reachable at 2× by choosing a smaller output format, that beats reaching it at 4× with the best possible kernel by a factor of three in surviving detail.

Can sharpening recover what interpolation lost?

The obvious next move after a soft upscale is to sharpen it. Measured on the same two high-frequency classes (text/UI and line art), Lanczos upscale followed by an unsharp mask, scored against the same ground truth:

FactorContentPlainBest sharpeningGap closed
2×Text / UI39.63%75.24%59.0%
2×Line art36.47%73.89%58.9%
4×Text / UI6.55%17.63%11.9%
4×Line art10.05%21.84%13.1%

At 2× a heavy unsharp mask recovers roughly six tenths of what interpolation threw away. At 4× the identical operation recovers one eighth. Sharpening is not rescuing detail — it is amplifying the edge energy that is there, and at 4× there is very little left to amplify.

The side effect scales the other way, and it is the one that shows up in finished work. Ink coverage against ground truth, on line art:

Post-opInk at 2×Δ vs GTInk at 4×Δ vs GT
Lanczos, no sharpening4.15%−5.513.00%−6.66
unsharp r1 100%15.16%+5.514.49%−5.17
unsharp r2 150%15.28%+5.635.74%−3.92

Ground truth is 9.66% ink. At 2× the unsharp mask does not restore the missing ink — it doubles it, taking a 5.5-point deficit to a 5.5-point surplus. The strokes do not come back; a halo of near-black pixels appears next to them and the ink counter cannot tell the difference. At 4× the mask never even reaches parity: 5.74% against a 9.66% target, still 41% short after the heaviest setting tested.

So the ranking of knobs holds after post-processing too. Sharpening is a correction applied after the information has already been destroyed, and its recovery rate falls from ~59% to ~12% as the factor doubles. The honest summary of the factorial and this follow-up: choose the smallest scale factor your output format allows, then choose content-appropriate sharpening; the interpolation kernel is close to irrelevant next to both.

Reproduce it

import numpy as np
from PIL import Image

def hf_fraction(a):
    a = a.astype(np.float64); a -= a.mean()
    mag = np.abs(np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fft2(a)))
    h, w = mag.shape
    yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:h, 0:w]
    r = np.hypot(yy - h/2, xx - w/2)
    return mag[r > 0.18*w].sum() / mag[r <= 0.18*w].sum()

gt  = np.asarray(Image.open('source.png').convert('L'), np.float32)
ref = hf_fraction(gt)
for f in (2, 3, 4):
    src = np.asarray(Image.fromarray(gt.astype(np.uint8))
                     .resize((gt.shape[1]//f, gt.shape[0]//f), Image.BOX))
    for k in ('BILINEAR', 'BICUBIC', 'LANCZOS'):
        up = np.asarray(Image.fromarray(src).resize((gt.shape[1], gt.shape[0]),
                                                    getattr(Image, k)), np.float32)
        print(f, k, round(100 * hf_fraction(up) / ref, 2), '%')

One honest caveat on the ratio. Retention is a ratio against each source's own high-frequency energy, so smooth content scores higher by construction — the portrait class keeps 21.27% at 4× partly because its ground truth has very little high frequency to lose. That is why the variance share above is computed on log retention (which compresses the ratio) and why the product-edge and text numbers, not the portrait numbers, are the ones to carry into a decision about hard-edged content.

Method notes

  • HF retention — ratio of 2-D FFT magnitude outside a radius of 0.18 × image width to the magnitude inside it. Not a standard metric; built because PSNR and SSIM both failed to distinguish "smooth" from "detailed" in a way that matched the eye. Compare methods on the same image, never across images.
  • PSNR — ffmpeg psnr filter, both inputs forced to the same size with a bicubic scale so the comparison is per-pixel.
  • Ink coverage — fraction of pixels below mid-grey (128). Appropriate for binary content, meaningless for photographs.
  • All tests — ffmpeg 7.x, numpy, PIL. No proprietary tools, no vendor benchmarks.
