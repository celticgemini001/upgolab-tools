AI Upscaling Notes

Measured guides to what upscaling actually does to an image — not what the preset names imply. Every number below came from ffmpeg and numpy on real files, and each page explains how to reproduce it.

The measurement that organises everything else

Interpolation methods ranked by PSNR are ranked almost exactly backwards by detail retention. On a 4× upscale of a Siemens-star test pattern:

MethodPSNRHF detail retained
nearest24.60 dB25.4%
bilinear25.68 dB3.9%
bicubic26.27 dB2.1%
lanczos26.47 dB1.3%

At 2× instead of 4×, Lanczos retains 21.5% instead of 1.3%. Halving the scale factor gains roughly 16× more surviving detail — far more than any choice of kernel. That single fact reframes most upscaling decisions.

Stretching One Axis: Anisotropic Upscale, Measured

Every guide quotes a single multiplier, but real jobs are often lopsided. The widely repeated +2.2 dB for touching one axis turns out to be a source-pixel budget artefact: that comparison gave the single-axis case twice the input pixels. At equal pixels the two-axis case wins by 4.3 dB on isotropic content, and the sign flips at anisotropy 0.40 — one axis wins by up to 16 dB when the detail sits on the other one. 8-bit vs 10-bit: Why Banding Widens When You Upscale

An 8-bit gradient upscaled 4x produces 16-pixel flat plateaus, and plateau width is exactly width/255 at every scale factor — 4.00, 8.00, 16.00, 32.00 px measured against 4.02, 8.03, 16.06, 32.13 predicted. Dither collapses that to 2.00 px but costs 37% worse RMSE, because rounding is the optimal quantiser by definition. A 16-bit master does nothing once you deliver 8-bit: identical 16.00 px. And 10-bit H.264 came out smaller at every CRF tested, not 20-50% larger.

plateau = width/255 · dither +37% RMSE · 10-bit smaller at CRF 12-28

JPEG Block Seams Don't Get Amplified by Upscaling — They Get Wider

The seam keeps a constant 1.26× contrast against its surroundings at 1×, 2×, 4× and 8×; what grows is the area, as k². Deblocking after the upscale beat deblocking before it on both PSNR and seam ratio. Blind 8-grid detection found the phase on 4/4 crops — and the clean control showed R spans 0.668–1.364 with no JPEG present at all, so a raw R above 1 proves nothing. Sharpening did not selectively amplify the seam.

seam ratio 1.26× at every scale · deblock after wins · jpeg = 8.8% of error at 4×

Re-Shoot or Upscale? The Break-Even Point, Measured

A real 1080×1620 capture downscaled and put back: a 4× upscale is 27.2 dB from the original, each doubling costs 4–5 dB, and nearest-neighbour beats Lanczos on high-frequency energy by 6.9× while losing 2.4 dB of PSNR. Filter both images to what the eye resolves and the same 4× scores 32.5 dB at arm's length and 40.3 dB from two metres, which is what turns the argument into a viewing-distance table. Three negative results, including resampling amplifying noise below one 8-bit level.

27.2 → 32.5 dB with distance · chip 0.09 dB · crop +7.8 dB

Tiled Upscaling Seams: How Much Context a Tile Needs

Zero-context tiling put 0.45% of output pixels more than 2 levels off, and 67.6% of those sat within 2 px of a tile boundary — a 21× concentration inside 3.18% of the frame. Three source pixels of context made 54, 64, 108, 180, 256 and 270-pixel tiles all bit-identical to the single-shot result, while blending overlapping tiles was measurably worse than cropping the context back off. Tile size turned out not monotonic: 270-pixel tiles scored 8.65 dB worse on seams than 180-pixel ones with half the seam area. Two instrument bugs found and reported.

0.45% off · 3 px = exact · blend −0.7 level · 34 dB for per-tile auto-levels

Fractional Upscale Factors: Why 1.25× Can Lose to 1.333×

Real targets are rarely 2×. Seven exact rational factors measured on one photograph: the larger factor beat the smaller one, 4/3 over 5/4 by 0.64 dB in five crops of five, while gradient and HF retention stayed perfectly monotonic. The anomaly vanished under Lanczos and bicubic downscaling and appeared only under an aliasing BOX decimator — so the ranking was measuring the downscaler. Step-chaining lost 0.30–0.70 dB at every split, and sharpening paid +0.055 dB at 100% gradient retention against +0.375 dB at 88%.

5/5 crops · chain −0.70 dB · USM 0.05 → 0.38 dB

Gamma Space vs Linear Light: Does sRGB Resampling Cost Quality?

The standard demonstration is real: a 50/50 checkerboard area-averaged in sRGB returns 127/255 where physics demands 187/255 — 60 code values. But on a real photo the same linear pipeline scores 0.98 dB worse, and the winner reverses when PSNR is computed in linear light instead of gamma. Pure upscaling with no downscale moves the result by 0.40 cv — nothing. The colour space of your metric decides the winner, which makes most published comparisons uninterpretable.

−60 cv in gamma · winner flips with the metric

Sharpen Before or After Upscaling? The Order Survives Float, Not a File

The two orders are the same operation in float — matched radius, 0.002 dB apart. They only diverge when a quantiser sits between them: one 8-bit step costs 0.138 dB, a JPEG intermediate costs up to 0.549 dB, and a linear-light resampler breaks the equivalence on its own terms. Sharpening after wins 12/12 across four photographs at 2×, 3× and 4×, but by only 0.100 dB — and pre-sharpening is better on flat regions.

0.002 dB in float · 12/12 favour "after" · 1.80× faster "before"

Which knob actually decides whether detail survives?

The guides above each change one thing at a time. That is fine for explaining a mechanism and useless for ranking causes, because the knobs interact. So the next test ran a full factorial: 3 scale factors × 3 kernels × 4 content classes = 36 cells, on 1024×1024 sources. Each source was downscaled with a box filter (no ringing introduced before the test), then upscaled back and scored for high-frequency retention against its own ground truth.

The point of a factorial is not the 36 numbers. It is that you can attribute the variance between them to a cause. Taking the main-effect sum of squares on log retention:

Source of variationLevelsShare of variance
Scale factor (2× / 3× / 4×)370.1%
Content class (text, line art, portrait, product edge)417.3%
Kernel (bilinear / bicubic / lanczos)30.1%

Choosing the kernel — the decision every upscaling UI puts first, in a dropdown — accounts for one part in a thousand of the variation in surviving detail. The scale factor accounts for seven hundred times more. Content class sits in between, which is why presets named after content ("photo", "anime", "text") beat presets named after filters.

Mean high-frequency retention by content class and factor:

Content class2×3×4×
Text / UI37.43%22.60%7.47%
Line art30.01%16.27%8.37%
Portrait-like36.81%24.82%21.27%
Product edge28.00%14.14%7.13%
All cells33.06%19.46%11.06%

The kernel ranking flips, which is why it cannot be trusted

If kernel mattered, the best kernel would be the best kernel. Across the twelve cells at each factor, averaged:

FactorbilinearbicubiclanczosWinner
2×28.02%35.98%35.19%bicubic
3×20.43%19.55%18.38%bilinear
4×11.92%10.68%10.57%bilinear

Bicubic wins at 2× by 2.4 points and loses at 4× by 1.3. The ordering is not a property of the kernel; it is a property of the kernel at that factor on that content. Every "which interpolation is best" comparison that fixes one factor is reporting an interaction term as if it were a main effect.

The interaction is visible directly as the spread between kernels inside a single cell. At 2× the spread reaches 10.27 points on text; at 4× the same content has only 2.37 points of kernel spread. The higher the factor, the less the filter choice can matter, because there is less information left to preserve differently.

Two results that contradict the usual advice

RMSE does not track detail retention. Correlating log retention against RMSE across all 36 cells gives r = −0.30. A weaker relationship than that is hard to produce from numbers measuring the same images. At 4× on line art, lanczos has the lowest RMSE (66.22) and the highest retention (8.66%); at 4× on product edges, bilinear has both the highest RMSE (6.65) and the highest retention (9.19%). If you rank methods by error you will pick differently depending on the content, which means the metric is not measuring what the eye uses.

Step size beats every other choice, and the curve is not linear. Going 3× → 4× costs a mean of 8.4 points of retention (19.46% → 11.06%); going 2× → 3× costs 13.6 points. The first doubling is the expensive one. Practical reading: if a target size is reachable at 2× by choosing a smaller output format, that beats reaching it at 4× with the best possible kernel by a factor of three in surviving detail.

Can sharpening recover what interpolation lost?

The obvious next move after a soft upscale is to sharpen it. Measured on the same two high-frequency classes (text/UI and line art), Lanczos upscale followed by an unsharp mask, scored against the same ground truth:

FactorContentPlainBest sharpeningGap closed
2×Text / UI39.63%75.24%59.0%
2×Line art32.82%67.32%51.4%
4×Text / UI6.55%17.63%11.9%
4×Line art8.66%18.71%11.0%

At 2× a heavy unsharp mask recovers half to six tenths of what interpolation threw away. At 4× the identical operation recovers about one eighth. Sharpening is not rescuing detail — it is amplifying the edge energy that is there, and at 4× there is very little left to amplify.

The side effect scales the other way, and it is the one that shows up in finished work. Ink coverage against ground truth, on line art:

Post-opInk at 2×Δ vs GTInk at 4×Δ vs GT
Lanczos, no sharpening4.19%−5.533.00%−6.72
unsharp r1 100%15.22%+5.494.49%−5.23
unsharp r2 150%15.38%+5.665.78%−3.95

Ground truth is 9.72% ink. At 2× the unsharp mask does not restore the missing ink — it multiplies it by 3.6, taking a 5.5-point deficit to a 5.5-point surplus. The strokes do not come back; a halo of near-black pixels appears next to them and the ink counter cannot tell the difference. At 4× the mask never even reaches parity: 5.78% against a 9.72% target, still 41% short after the heaviest setting tested.

So the ranking of knobs holds after post-processing too. Sharpening is a correction applied after the information has already been destroyed, and its recovery rate falls from 51–59% to 11–12% as the factor doubles. The honest summary of the factorial and this follow-up: choose the smallest scale factor your output format allows, then choose content-appropriate sharpening; the interpolation kernel is close to irrelevant next to both.

Reproduce it

import numpy as np
from PIL import Image

def hf_fraction(a):
    a = a.astype(np.float64); a -= a.mean()
    mag = np.abs(np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fft2(a)))
    h, w = mag.shape
    yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:h, 0:w]
    r = np.hypot(yy - h/2, xx - w/2)
    return mag[r > 0.18*w].sum() / mag[r <= 0.18*w].sum()

gt  = np.asarray(Image.open('source.png').convert('L'), np.float32)
ref = hf_fraction(gt)
for f in (2, 3, 4):
    src = np.asarray(Image.fromarray(gt.astype(np.uint8))
                     .resize((gt.shape[1]//f, gt.shape[0]//f), Image.BOX))
    for k in ('BILINEAR', 'BICUBIC', 'LANCZOS'):
        up = np.asarray(Image.fromarray(src).resize((gt.shape[1], gt.shape[0]),
                                                    getattr(Image, k)), np.float32)
        print(f, k, round(100 * hf_fraction(up) / ref, 2), '%')

# the sharpening test, same source, same scorer
from PIL import ImageFilter
for f, pct, rad in ((2, 200, 1), (4, 150, 2)):
    src = np.asarray(Image.fromarray(gt.astype(np.uint8))
                     .resize((gt.shape[1]//f, gt.shape[0]//f), Image.BOX))
    up = Image.fromarray(src).resize((gt.shape[1], gt.shape[0]), Image.LANCZOS).convert('L')
    sh = up.filter(ImageFilter.UnsharpMask(radius=rad, percent=pct, threshold=0))
    print(f, 'sharpened', round(100 * hf_fraction(np.asarray(sh, np.float32)) / ref, 2), '%')

One honest caveat on the ratio. Retention is a ratio against each source's own high-frequency energy, so smooth content scores higher by construction — the portrait class keeps 21.27% at 4× partly because its ground truth has very little high frequency to lose. That is why the variance share above is computed on log retention (which compresses the ratio) and why the product-edge and text numbers, not the portrait numbers, are the ones to carry into a decision about hard-edged content.

Method notes

Halftone Dots Under Upscaling: The Lattice Scales Exactly

A screened print's dot lattice scales with the scale factor to within 0.56%, but killing the dots with a Gaussian costs an edge blur of 1.1-1.3x the dot pitch. Nearest-neighbour clumps 3.2x worse at fractional factors, and below 2 output pixels per cell the dots simply stop existing.

pitch error 0.56% · descreen cost 1.1-1.3p · dots vanish under 2 px/cell

Noise and Grain Under Upscaling: Amplitude Down, Clumping Up

Upscaling does not amplify noise — bilinear cuts its amplitude to 0.66x. What it does is concentrate the remainder into clumps 2.6x wider than the source pixels, and denoising before the upscale loses in 12/12 cases until the frame is above sigma 3.

gain 0.66–0.89x · clump 2.62 px · pre-denoise wins 0/12 below σ 3

HDR Upscaling: What 16-bit Actually Buys You

A known ripple through 4× down and 4× up, quantised to 8 and 16 bits. Detail survives at 100% in 16-bit and 0% in 8-bit above display code 253 — and the two are identical after a +2 stop lift. Grading before the tone map beats both.

0% vs 100% above code 253 · 0.00 dB at +2 stops · 19.49 stops in 8-bit
  • HF retention — ratio of 2-D FFT magnitude outside a radius of 0.18 × image width to the magnitude inside it. Not a standard metric; built because PSNR and SSIM both failed to distinguish "smooth" from "detailed" in a way that matched the eye. Compare methods on the same image, never across images.
  • PSNR — ffmpeg psnr filter, both inputs forced to the same size with a bicubic scale so the comparison is per-pixel.
  • Ink coverage — fraction of pixels below mid-grey (128). Appropriate for binary content, meaningless for photographs.
  • All tests — ffmpeg 7.x, numpy, PIL. No proprietary tools, no vendor benchmarks.
